Comment on “Copyrights and Creativity: Evidence from
                 Italian Opera in the Napoleonic Age”

                                        Marios Tsoukis
                                               NYU

                                      February 11, 2026




                                             Abstract

   In their 2020 Journal of Political Economy article, Giorcelli and Moser study the Napoleonic
invasion of Italy to find evidence of the effects of copyright laws on opera production. I document
internal inconsistencies in the replication package and differences with the econometric procedures
described in the paper. Further, I use publicly accessible data on opera premieres to replicate
their findings, failing to recover their main results. Finally, I discuss the core elements of the
historical setting studied by the authors, finding that the core tenets of the identification strategy
are not supported by the historical literature, nor by primary sources.




                                                  1
1     Introduction

    In their 2020 Journal of Political Economy article “Copyrights and Creativity: Evidence from
Italian Opera in the Napoleonic Age”, Giorcelli and Moser (2020), henceforth GM2020, investigate
the causal effects of copyright laws on creative output, using Italian opera production and an exogenous
change in copyright law in parts of Italy. The paper finds that opera quantity and quality increase
post-copyright introduction. They find no negative effect on opera production in untreated states, and
a positive effect on Italian émigré composers returning to Italy. Their results suggest that copyright
protection increases both the quantity and quality of innovation.

    I fail to replicate some of the core findings using the replication package provided, and I document
both internal inconsistencies within the datasets included in the replication package and with publicly
available datasets. When attempting to reconstruct the main variables on opera quality using the
source cited in GM2020, I fail to obtain the same results, and I document that the dataset on an
alternate metric of opera quality GM2020 employ is perfectly correlated with their main variable on
opera quantity.

    Moreover, the analysis in GM2020 rests on three claims about the historical setting which underpin
the identification strategy: (1) In 1801, the French copyright law of 1793 was extended to two regions
of Italy, Lombardy and Venetia. (2) The other regions of Italy, which eventually also came under
Napoleonic control, did not introduce similar laws. (3) The copyright laws enacted in Lombardy and
Venetia remained intact following the Austrian Empire gaining control over the territories in 1815.
My analysis of the historical evidence does not support these core tenets of the identification strategy,
and I fail to find some of the primary sources GM2020 adduce to support their interpretation.



2     GM2020 Replication Package

    The paper’s replication package, available online, provides datasets and Stata codes to replicate
their results. Their replication package spans 22 datasets, 5 of which include the number of operas
produced across different aggregations and used in the main paper. Each exhibit has a separate file,
and often a separate, already cleaned dataset. The datasets published are post-collection; they do not
show the scripts needed to recreate them from the raw data. The raw opera data are not included
in the package, and the authors refused to share them and the intermediate scripts upon request.
Operas are not named, only tallied, and the authors assign a composer id to each composer without
identifying them.

    Overall, GM2020 list five books used to compile their dataset. These are: Carlo Dassori’s Opere e
Operisti, Dizionario Lirico (1903), Loewenberg’s (1978) Annals of Opera, Corrado Ambiveri’s (1998)

                                                   2
Opere e Operisti Minori dell’Ottocento Italiano, New Grove Dictionary of Music and Musicians
(2001), Treccani’s Enciclopedia Italiana di scienze, lettere ed arti (2001). The authors treat operas
included in Loewenberg (1978) as of higher quality; Loewenberg includes only notable opera premieres.
Loewenberg is also the most restrictive source of their sample “133 of 1,353 operas in Dassori (1903)
and none of 71 operas in Ambiveri (1998) are included in Loewenberg (1978)” (GM2020, p. 16). The
authors report 705 Italian-born composers who premiere 2598 operas between 1770 and 1900. For the
period 1781 to 1820, they report 677 operas by 192 composers. The number of new operas is strictly
positive for every state-year observation.

2.1.1   Composer Data

   GM2020 find that opera production increases overall, and that production per composer goes up.
They use the composer level data.dta to support their claims. The dataset in the GM2020 replication
package references 192 Italian-born composers, as opposed to the 264 I find in the ON database. The
composers in their replication package are not named, but rather have a unique id number. These
range from 1–210, with 18 IDs missing. 73% (141/192) of composers only compose a single opera.
There is a column with individual compositions per state per year for each composer operas comp,
and a column with a fixed value per composer tot operas. There are 11 composers who have more
operas listed than reported as their overall opera compositions; the sum of operas comp is greater
than tot operas. They composed 47 more operas. This discrepancy is precisely the difference between
operas 1781 1820 and composer level data.

   Further, composer id = 13 is active in years 1781 through 1804, before abruptly producing an
extra 9 operas in 1813, all in Lombardy. In GM2020’s main opera count however, Lombardy only
produced 6 operas that year. Similarly, composer 8 composed 8 operas in Lombardy in 1814, composer
9 composed 9 operas in Lombardy in 1815, and composer 3 composed 8 operas in Lombardy in 1812.
For the above years, each composer is the only active composer in Lombardy. This is not reflected
in the alternative data on opera premieres I introduce in section 4.1, where multiple composers are
active in the same state each year, and where the most productive composer in a single year (Domenico
Cimarosa in 1781, 1782, 1783, 1784) composed six operas. Overall, there are 8 state-year-composer
outputs that exceed six operas. These are all in Lombardy, and post-treatment. Further, the data
for Venetia also exhibit inconsistencies. Venetia in 1803 has composers creating 11 operas, while their

   Returning Migrant Composers

   GM2020 claim that the introduction of copyrights induced Italian composers who were working
in France and Austria to return to Italy — specifically Lombardy and Venetia.                       Two datasets,

3      Econometric Issues

    In this section, I examine the econometric implementation used in GM2020. Overall, I find
inconsistencies between the analyses GM2020 claims to perform, and what the replication package
actually implements.

smallest unit of observation is the state-year, and they do not cluster errors at a coarser level.

       the level of states and years.”

3.3      Pre-trends

    To deal with issues relating to different pre-trends across the states, GM2020 control for them in
some specifications. In the operas 1781 − 1820.dta file, there are eight variables meant to account for
this. For each state s, the variable pre − trend s takes the values 1, 2, . . . , 20 for the years 1781–1800,
and 0 thereafter. Thus, within each state, pre − trend s increases linearly during the pre-treatment
period and then drops to zero at the start of the treatment period.

    This construction of the pre-trend variables is econometrically invalid. Each value for the variable
pre − trend s increases by one each year until 1800 before collapsing to zero in 1801, introducing a
discontinuity in the regressor at the treatment threshold. Consequently, pre − trend s mechanically
captures part of the treatment break. The regression therefore cannot separately identify a true

                                                                  10
treatment effect from the artificial jump embedded in the pre − trend s variable.



4     Replication

    I attempt to replicate the paper’s main results and figures on opera quantity using the “Opening
Night! Opera & Oratorio Premieres” (henceforth ON) data and on opera quality by recreating
their data on notable opera performances as identified by Loewenberg (1978); I fail to get similar
conclusions. Specifically, I investigate the main claims that the number of operas premiered increases
in Lombardy and Venetia following the claimed introduction of copyrights in 1801, that Lombardy
and Venetia were on similar pre-trends to the remainder of the states, that the results are robust to
excluding Milan and Venice, and that opera quality increases in Lombardy and Venetia following the
introduction of copyrights in 1801.


4.1    Opening Night! Premieres Databases

    The main dataset I use to replicate their findings is the ON dataset, which is based on the 2003
book “From Don to Giovanni: Opera and Oratorio Citation Database”, created by Richard Parrillo.
It is curated by Stanford University Libraries, and created in 2013; it was available before the paper’s
first draft in 2014, and the authors claim to use the dataset for their section 8 on other musical
compositions, not for the main analysis on opera quality or quantity. The data can be accessed using
the Julia package ON Operas.jl. I choose the ON database because the authors had access to it, as
they cite it as a source for their data on other musical compositions, and because of the accessibility
and ease of scraping. The data I compile using the ON database has 1876 operas by 264 distinct
Italian-born composers. For 46 state-year observations, there are no opera premieres by Italian-born
composers in the Italian cities GM2020 investigate. I attach a full list in Appendix B.1.

    GM2020 use the ON database to create the variable opening night in the dataset
musical compositions 1781 1820.dta. The variable is explained in Appendix table A18: “The dependent
variables Opening Night!, symphonies, and songs per state and year measures the number of new
operettas, oratorios, and serenades (excluding operas) in state i and year t between 1781 and 1820.”
The introduction of operettas as a genre, however, did not occur until Jacques Offenbach staged
the first operettas in Paris in the 1850s (Encyclopædia Britannica 2024). Further, 149 of the 320
observations of the opening night variable contain fractions. Overall, summing over all the states and
years, we get 729.5 operettas in Italy from 1781–1820, when operettas had yet to be invented. The
ON dataset does not identify any operettas in that time period. Additionally, while GM2020 identify
484 oratorios, ON only includes 164, and the source of the 2600 serenades is not clear.

4.3    Loewenberg and Noteworthy Operas

   I investigate one of their data sources, and find the data in the replication package presents a
significant discrepancy with the original source material. Specifically, I use Loewenberg (1978), as
this is the most easily accessible and is the sole source of their variable operas annals in the dataset
operas 1781 1820. This is used in the specifications of Table 4 of GM2020 to support the claim that
copyrights introduction improved not only quantity, but also quality.

   To recreate the correct Loewenberg data, I perform OCR on 147 pages and filter for performances
in cities in Italy and by Italian-born Composers. I manually check the OCR output. While GM2020
report 58 operas in the annals, I find 130 operas. The opera counts do not match for 37 of the 40
years; they only match for 1797, 1798, and 1800.

   The discrepancies between the original data in Loewenberg and the dataset in GM2020 are skewed
towards Lombardy and Venetia in the post-treatment period: in GM2020, 42% (24/58) of notable
operas were premiered post-treatment in these regions, compared with 30% (39/130) in Loewenberg.

5      Identification Strategy & Setting

    The paper exploits policy variation in the Napoleonic Wars in Italy to find variation in copyright
protection. The paper’s identification strategy rests on three points. First, that only Lombardy and
Venetia introduced copyright laws in 1801. Second, that they were the only states to have copyrights
laws until at least 1820. Third, that the copyright laws passed under Napoleonic rule remained after
the downfall of Napoleon and the subsequent Congress of Vienna in 1815. I investigate the historical
setting, and through examining primary and secondary sources, find that these three aspects of the
historical setting do not hold. I also critically assess the validity of the sources GM2020 cite.


5.1      Copyright Laws in Napoleonic Italy (ante 1815)

    The authors divide Italy into eight states following the 1815 borders drawn at the Congress of
Vienna. These are: the Kingdom of Lombardy (Lombardy), Venetia, the Kingdom of Sardinia
(Sardinia), the Duchy of Parma and Piacenza (Parma), the Duchy of Modena and Reggio (Modena),
the Grand Duchy of Tuscany (Tuscany), the Papal States, and the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies (Two
Sicilies). All of these states, either in their entirety or partially, enforced copyrights by 1811.

    The Sardinian territories in mainland Italy were annexed directly by France in 1802 followed by
former Duchy of Parma and Piacenza in 1808, the Grand Duchy of Tuscany in 1808, and the remainder
of the Papal States in 1809. These territories were ruled just like any other French territories, and as
such domestic French law would apply. This would extend to the copyrights law of 1793. I find an
extract of the 1813 Raccolta of the Département du Pô (Appendix A.5) where there is direct reference
to the French 1793 copyrights law. 5

    Southern Italy, with the exclusion of the island of Sicily, was organized into the French-controlled
Kingdom of Naples. In 1811, Decree 1134 was introduced (Appendix A.3). It introduces copyright
protection in the same way as the law GM2020 study, and explicitly includes theatrical performances,
such as operas.

5.2      Copyright Laws in Post-Napoleonic Italy (post 1815)

   Following the defeat of Napoleon and the subsequent Congress of Vienna (1815), France and
Napoleon lost control of the Italian mainland. GM2020 claim that copyright laws in Lombardy
and Venetia continued to apply. In fact, historical sources suggest that all Napoleonic laws relating
to publishing, including copyrights, were abolished when the Kingdom of Lombardy-Venetia was
controlled by the Habsburgs (Soresina 2018). Copyright laws are not introduced again until the
1830s.

   In 1816, the Kingdom of Lombardy–Venetia enacted the ‘Piano generale di censura’ — the censorship
master plan, explicitly repealing all Napoleonic laws which regulated publishing (see Appendix A.4
for further discussion).

   Rouvier (2024), explicitly discussing opera performances, confirms that the Kingdom of Lombardy–Venetia
did not enforce copyright laws in the immediate aftermath of the 1815 Congress of Vienna, and that
both composers and librettists received no extra remuneration beyond the original commission.

    Overall

    The historical evidence suggests a different timeline of copyright adoption than the one used in the
paper. Specifically, Venetia is erroneously classified as belonging to the Cisalpine Republic in 1801,
and Modena is erroneously classified as not being part of the Cisalpine Republic. Further, GM2020
does not take into account that the territories of the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies that were not
on the island of Sicily were organized into the Kingdom of Naples which introduced copyright laws.
Additionally, GM2020 overlook that the remainder of the states were directly annexed by France,
and consequently applied French laws. Moreover, the Italian re-organization of the theater extends
beyond the introduction of copyright protections. Censorship laws were changed, the arts were used
to foster a common revolutionary identity (Poggi (2021), Poggi (2024)), and gambling was legalized
in certain theaters (Bettin (2010)), leading to revenues increasing.



6     Conclusion

    In conclusion, I raise issues which question the credibility of the results of GM2020. This is
for three reasons. First, data inconsistencies across the replication package datasets, econometric
misspecifications and incorrect Stata implementations of econometric procedures undermine the credibility
of results. Second, their results cannot be replicated by external data sources. Third, the historical
setting is not accurately portrayed, and it is not appropriate for establishing causal effects of copyright
on opera production. As the authors refused to share their raw data and the sources of some of their
data and some of their historical evidence, I am unable to locate the source of the discrepancies. This
report therefore highlights the value of completely publicly available replication packages for data
processing and collection.

   (No. 112.) DECREE extending to the united Venetian departments the provisions of Law 19
Floril, Year IX, which grant exclusive rights to authors of intellectual creations.

   June 27, 1806.
                                                             33
   NAPOLEON I,

   By the grace of God and the Constitutions, EMPEROR OF THE FRENCH and KING OF ITALY:

   EUGENE NAPOLEON OF FRANCE, Vice-King of Italy, Prince of Venice, Archchancellor of
State of the French Empire, to all who see these presents, greetings.

   We; by virtue of the authority delegated to us by the Most High and Most August Emperor and
King NAPOLEON I, our most honored Father and gracious Sovereign;

   On the report of the Minister of the Interior dated May 12, 1806;

   Recognizing the need to also apply to the united departments the provisions of Law 19 Floril Year
IX (May 9, 1801), which guarantee authors exclusive rights to their works;

   Having seen Law 19 Floril Year IX, Having consulted the Council of State,

   We have decreed and hereby decree the following:

   Art. I. Law 19 Floril Year IX is published and implemented in the united departments.

   II. Printers of both the united departments and other departments who, prior to the publication
of this Decree, have begun or completed the reprinting of works that enjoyed exclusive rights in their
respective States, must within fifteen days submit to their respective Prefectures the list and relevant
proofs of the reprints already begun or completed before the aforementioned period, which will be
submitted to the Minister of the Interior.

   III. With the exception of these reprints for all privileged works, the provisions and exclusive rights
that Law 19 Fiorile Year IX attributes to authors of intellectual works shall apply in all departments
of the Kingdom.

   The Minister of the Interior is responsible for the execution of this Decree, which will be published
and included in the Bulletin of Laws.

   EUGENE NAPOLEON.

   For the Viceroy,

   The Secretary of State,

   L. VACCARi

B.2 Notable performances as classified by Loewenberg (1978)

   Loewenberg (1978) lists operas chronologically, by date of premiere. It names the opera, the
composer, the city, and the venue the opera was premiered. Additionally, it lists cities and years
in which the opera was also shown in following seasons, but it does not contain information on the
count of how many times each opera was performed. I perform OCR on the book, and then filter
for performances by Italian-born composers and premieres in Italy. Further, I verify and check the
output.

   GM2020 correctly represent what Loewenberg lists for three years, 1797, 1798, 1800. For all other
years, either the number of operas premiered in Lombardy and Venetia, or the number of operas
premiered in other states does not match, or both.

   Below I show the entry for 1783, found on pages 401-407. GM2020 report no operas recorded in
Loewenberg for this year. I highlight the 7 operas named that were performed in Italy, by Italian-born
composers, namely: “Il Conte di Bell’Umore” by Bernardini in Rome, “Chi dell’altrui si veste, presto
si spoglia” by Cimarosa in Naples, “I due Baroni di Rocca Azzurra” by Cimarosa in Rome, “La Quakera
spiritosa” by Gugliemi in Naples, “Lo Sposo di tre, Marito di Nessuna” by Cherubini in Venice, “La
Villanella rapita” by Bianchi in Venice, and “Gli Amanti alla Prova” by Caruso in Venice.

                                                  60
The remainder of the paragraph is dedicated to explaining how copyrights applied to musical
compositions in the context of theater (including opera). I provide the translation of the original
using Google Translate. I add emphasis on what I believe to be the relevant section:

     The problem is easily solved by admitting an analogous application of §§ 1164, 1165 and
     1166. The texts of the norms read as follows: § 1164. ‘‘By means of a contract for
     the publication of a book, the author gives someone the right to print and sell
     it. By this contract, the author renounces the right to give the same book to
     others for publication”; § 1165. “The author must deliver the work according to the
     contract, and the publisher must pay the author the agreed compensation as soon as the
     work is delivered to him” and § 1166. “If the work is not delivered by the author within the
     established time or in the agreed manner, the publisher can withdraw from the contract,
     and if the delivery of the work is omitted due to the author’s fault, he can also demand
     compensation (. . . )”. It is very easy to imagine what results could be achieved
     by replacing the term “publisher” with that of “impresario”: we are in fact
     faced with the clear modality of action of the author/impresario relationship
     with the commission by the latter, through a contract, of a work from the
     artist and the subsequent staging of this by the client, who is the theatrical
     correspondent of the book edition.

Consequently, the discussion of copyrights included in Bassi (2000) does not support the claims made
in GM2020. The copyright lies with the impresario, not with the composer, and there are no legal
obligations for compensation for repeat performances.
